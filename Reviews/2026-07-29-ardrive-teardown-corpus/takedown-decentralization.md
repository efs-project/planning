# ArDrive Teardown — Lane: Content Moderation, Takedown Reality, and Centralization Map

Researched 2026-07-29. All sources accessed 2026-07-29 unless a publication date is given. Layer discipline maintained throughout: **Arweave** = storage chain, **ArFS** = filesystem protocol, **ArDrive** = the shipped product (operated by Permanent Data Solutions, Inc. / the AR.IO team).

## Summary

The "permanent, censorship-resistant" story and the takedown story are both true, because they happen at different layers — and ArDrive/AR.IO is unusually honest about this. Data written to Arweave cannot be deleted by anyone, including the company (their ToS says so in all caps), but it can be made *hard to reach*: the arweave.net gateway (operated by the ar.io team since ~2022) complies with DMCA notices by purging content from its caches and blocklisting uploading wallets, every AR.IO gateway ships with a per-operator blocklist admin API, and ArDrive-the-company reports CSAM to NCMEC and delists content identifiers from infrastructure it operates. Nothing network-wide exists: a blocked file remains fetchable from any non-blocking gateway or directly from miners. The legally compellable surface is exactly the corporate surface: PDS Inc. (Delaware/NJ, US law, DMCA agent) plus each gateway operator in their own jurisdiction; miners are compellable only individually, and no court precedent forcing Arweave-level deletion was found. On centralization: ArDrive-the-product routes essentially everything through company infrastructure by default — Turbo bundler/payment service (open-source AGPL but hosted by the company; credits non-refundable, expire in 3 years, held in a company ledger), arweave.net as default gateway and GraphQL index (with Goldsky as the other major indexer), and the centrally hosted app.ardrive.io (though permaweb-deployed app versions exist). The AR.IO network (mainnet Feb 2025, ~582 gateways listed on the network portal) plus the Wayfinder ar:// routing/verification stack is a genuine, shipping attempt to dilute the arweave.net single point of failure — more credible than most web3 decentralization theater, but traffic and defaults still concentrate on company endpoints.

## Findings

### 1. The takedown chain — what actually happens when someone demands removal

**The layers respond differently, by design.** A takedown demand against a public ArDrive file can land at four places: (a) Arweave miners storing the chunks, (b) gateways serving and indexing them, (c) ArDrive/Turbo the company, (d) the app UI. Only (b) and (c) do anything in practice.

**Arweave's "content policy" doctrine is real but weak as a removal mechanism.** The protocol's stance is voluntarism: each miner chooses what to store and serve, and can load content policies (the Arweave team ships an NSFW filter; the community "Shepherd" tool lets node operators build their own screening policies, per the [Arweave mining guide](https://docs.arweave.org/developers/mining/mining-guide)). Network-wide rejection of a specific file requires a majority of nodes to independently refuse it — there is no coordination mechanism, no registry of what miners actually filter, and no published data on adoption of content policies. As a removal path for a specific aggrieved party, it is effectively theoretical: you cannot petition "Arweave" to drop a file, and I found no documented case of the miner-level policy mechanism being used to purge specific content network-wide. Its real function is prophylactic (miners screening CSAM at ingest to limit their own liability).

**Gateways are where takedowns actually happen — and arweave.net's practices are documented by the operator itself.** A May 2023 article by JonnieSparkles (then COO of PDS Inc., ArDrive's operating company) describes arweave.net's practice: on a DMCA notice or malicious-content report, they "purge it from the gateway's caches and ensure that it is no longer served," and repeat-offender wallets are blocklisted — the gateway stops forwarding *future* transactions from that wallet to the network (a write-side punishment, not just read-side). He is explicit that "no data is lost in these scenarios, only temporarily inaccessible" — the data can still be retrieved "by searching the network, downloading data chunks, and assembling them into the desired file" directly from miners ([Guarding the Gates, Medium, 2023-05-18](https://jonniesparkles.medium.com/guarding-the-gates-d0eae0a4a313)). The article also names the pressures forcing compliance: domain de-listing by the DNS provider and ISP-level blocking if they don't act.

**Documented collateral-damage incidents — the real-world pain is mostly *over*-blocking, not under-blocking:**
- **Shared-wallet blocklisting.** Platforms that uploaded many users' data through one wallet had the entire wallet blocklisted after one user uploaded copyrighted material — all users' data items became unreachable through arweave.net until resolved (same Guarding the Gates article). This is the closest thing to a documented takedown precedent: DMCA-driven, gateway-level, with innocent-bystander damage.
- **Phishing-aggregator flagging of the whole domain.** Because anyone can upload phishing pages to Arweave, security vendors (Norton, Malwarebytes, iQ Abuse Scan) have repeatedly flagged **arweave.net itself** as a phishing/malware site, breaking NFT image loads for e.g. Manifold users and blocking whole ISPs' customers from the gateway ([Manifold forum thread](https://forum.manifold.xyz/t/arweave-is-blocked-listed-as-a-phishing-site/2970); [Malwarebytes forum](https://forums.malwarebytes.com/topic/310999-some-websites-used-to-access-arweave-flagged-as-phishingmalware/)). One user's abuse degrades every user's access — the flip side of shared infrastructure. AR.IO has responded with moderation tooling including a phishing-detection sidecar for gateways (rule-based + ML scoring, per [ar.io GitHub](https://github.com/ar-io)).

**AR.IO gateway moderation tooling is first-class, per-operator, and jurisdictional.** Every AR.IO gateway ships an admin API: `PUT /ar-io/admin/block-data` with an ID, optional `source` (name of a blocklist feed) and `notes` fields, protected by an admin key ([ar-io-node repo](https://github.com/ar-io/ar-io-node)). The official docs frame this as each gateway maintaining "distinct blocklists that reflect... the specific region that the gateway operates from" ([docs.ar.io gateways](https://docs.ar.io/learn/gateways); [ar.io gateway article](https://ar.io/articles/ar-io-gateways/)). Moderation is explicitly a *gateway-differentiation* feature, not a network mandate. Note the `source` field: the design anticipates shared/subscribed blocklist feeds, but I found no evidence of a network-wide mandatory feed.

**ArDrive-the-company (ToS, accessed 2026-07-29,** [ardrive.io/tos-and-privacy](https://ardrive.io/tos-and-privacy/)**):**
- Permanence is stated with unusual bluntness: "WE HAVE NO ABILITY TO DELETE FILES OR COMMUNICATIONS THAT YOU ADD TO THE PERMAWEB. YOUR UPLOADING OF ANY FILES TO THE PERMAWEB SHALL BE PERMANENT."
- DMCA (Section 20): "we cannot remove a file from the servers; however, if we receive a DMCA notice... we will work to have the data identifier to such content removed so that the content is not searchable or accessible via gateways that are operated by us." Registered process: legal@ardrive.io / mail to Clifton, NJ.
- CSAM (Section 8): "Zero Tolerance," reports to NCMEC and/or authorities, removal of "content identifiers so the content is not searchable or accessible via gateways that we operate."
- Monitoring: "We assume no responsibility for monitoring the Service for inappropriate content or conduct" — i.e., **reactive, not proactive** moderation.
- Termination: at discretion, without refund of prepaid service.

**Does Turbo scan uploads (CSAM hashing etc.)? Unverified.** I found no public statement that the Turbo upload service proactively scans content at ingest (no Safer/PhotoDNA-style disclosure), and the ToS's "no monitoring" language plus reactive NCMEC-reporting posture suggest it does not. The open-source [turbo-upload-service](https://github.com/ardriveapp/turbo-upload-service) README describes no scanning stage. Treat "Turbo does not scan uploads" as probable but unconfirmed. Turbo *can* refuse service to wallets (the blocklist mechanism), and the app's own encryption for private drives means the company could not scan private content anyway.

**Legal precedent: none found.** No court order compelling deletion from Arweave itself, and no documented government-takedown incident beyond routine DMCA/gateway blocking, surfaced in searches (2026-07-29). Absence of evidence, not proof — but after eight-plus years of the network's existence, the takedown reality is evidently being absorbed at the gateway/company layer without producing case law.

**Bottom line — unreachable vs. fetchable vs. compellable:**

| Action | Achievable? | Who can be compelled |
|---|---|---|
| Delete data from Arweave | No mechanism exists; even network-wide miner rejection is uncoordinated and undocumented in practice | Individual miners in their own jurisdictions (to stop *serving/storing their copies*), never the network |
| Make data unreachable via arweave.net + company gateways | Yes, routinely done (DMCA, phishing, CSAM) | PDS Inc. / ar.io team — US company, DMCA agent, NCMEC obligations |
| Make data unreachable via all AR.IO gateways | Only if every operator independently blocks; no global kill switch | Each gateway operator individually, per local law |
| Make data undiscoverable (GraphQL/index) | Yes at company indexes (arweave.net, Goldsky); the ArFS metadata pointing at the file stays on-chain | Index operators |
| Block a wallet from writing via Turbo/arweave.net | Yes (forwarding refusal) | The bundler/gateway operator; direct-to-miner submission remains open |

### 2. Centralization map — every company dependency in ArDrive-the-product

**Corporate spine.** ArDrive is operated by Permanent Data Solutions, Inc. (Delaware; NJ address in ToS), which also built AR.IO and Turbo — [pds.inc](https://pds.inc/) lists all three as its products. The AR.IO Network launched mainnet + the ARIO token on 2025-02-20 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/342404/arweave-based-permanent-cloud-storage-network-ar-io-launches-mainnet-and-tge); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/02/20/3029734/0/en/AR-IO-Launches-Mainnet-to-Power-First-Permanent-Cloud-Network.html)). The same team therefore ships the app, the default bundler, the default gateway, and stewards the naming system — a fully vertically integrated stack under decentralization branding. The pieces are genuinely open source (AGPL-3.0 turbo-upload-service, ar-io-node), which distinguishes this from lock-in, but operationally it is one company.

**Turbo bundler + payment service.** Turbo is the default write path in ArDrive (fiat/crypto → "Turbo Credits" → uploads; sub-100 KiB uploads free/company-subsidized, per [ar.io upload docs](https://docs.ar.io/build/upload/turbo-credits)). Key facts: credits are **non-refundable, non-transferable (share-only), and may expire 3 years after purchase** ([ArDrive Turbo help](https://help.ardrive.io/hc/en-us/articles/17193744715163-ArDrive-Turbo)); the credit ledger lives in the company's payment service, not on-chain. The upload service is open source and self-hostable but the hosted instance depends on PostgreSQL, S3, and SQS, and bundles must be posted to Arweave within a tx_anchor window (~50 min) ([turbo-upload-service repo](https://github.com/ardriveapp/turbo-upload-service)). **If Turbo dies:** already-settled data is safe on Arweave (DATA preserved); unspent credits are gone (money lost — custodial ledger, no refunds); the default WRITE path breaks until users fall back to direct wallet-funded uploads or another ANS-104 bundler (bundling is permissionless; AR.IO gateways can run their own bundler sidecar, per [docs.ar.io bundler](https://docs.ar.io/gateways/bundler)); data uploaded but not yet settled to Arweave in the optimistic window is at risk. ArDrive's "instant" upload UX is optimistic finality on company infrastructure.

**App hosting.** ardrive.io and app.ardrive.io are conventionally (centrally) hosted. The team also deploys app versions to the permaweb itself, reachable via ArNS/gateways (e.g. ardrive.ar.io — their own X handle brands it "ArDrive.ar.io"; their old "Dapp history of ArDrive" post described permaweb-hosted versions with full version history — that URL now 404s, itself a small object lesson in centralized-site rot vs. their permaweb copies). Losing app.ardrive.io loses the *convenient* client, not data and not the protocol: ArFS is open and third-party clients exist.

**Default gateway + GraphQL.** The app defaults to arweave.net for data and for its GraphQL index queries (drive/file/folder listings — ArFS is reconstructed entirely from GraphQL queries); users can change the gateway in settings ([help center](https://help.ardrive.io/hc/en-us/articles/12000559123995-Change-Arweave-Gateway-URL-for-ArDrive)). arweave.net has been operated under the ar.io team's custody since ~early 2022 ([ar.io Medium](https://medium.com/ar-io/what-is-ar-io-ca05303d1f7d)). The serious quiet centralization is **indexing**: GraphQL results are not consensus data; the practical index oligopoly is arweave.net + Goldsky's `arweave-search.goldsky.com` ([Goldsky](https://goldsky.com/chains/arweave); [permaweb cookbook](https://cookbook.arweave.net/fundamentals/gateways/graphql.html)). An index that omits a transaction makes a file invisible to ArDrive even though the bytes are on-chain — this is exactly where CSAM/DMCA delisting operates ("content identifiers removed... not searchable").

**ArNS registry.** ArNS names (used for sharing/hosting) live in the AR.IO network contract, an AO process ([ar.io on AO](https://ar.io/articles/ar-io-on-ao)). The whitepaper claims the contract is "immutable... without governance or special write access for its developers or the Foundation," upgradable only by ecosystem fork ([AR.IO whitepaper v2.1.0, 2025-02-18](https://whitepaper.ar.io/)). Take with salt: pricing was historically "flat fees defined by the core team," the AO substrate is young (AO mainnet also Feb 2025), and in practice the Foundation authored and shepherds every migration to date (SmartWeave → AO port was a team-executed state snapshot). Governance-by-fork is a real but heavy check.

**AR.IO gateway network in practice.** The network portal reported ~582 gateways and ~747 unique staking delegates ([gateways.ar.io](https://gateways.ar.io/), accessed 2026-07-29 via search snippet — treat the exact number as approximate). AR.IO claimed 1M+ monthly active users across gateways at mainnet. Independent operator count is respectable for web3 infra; what is *not* published is traffic share, and every default in the ArDrive product still points at arweave.net. **Wayfinder** (ar:// protocol: client-side gateway selection + cryptographic verification of returned data; SDK, browser extension, and a self-hostable router) is the shipping mitigation ([docs.ar.io wayfinder](https://docs.ar.io/learn/wayfinder); [wayfinder-router](https://github.com/ar-io/wayfinder-router)) — it directly addresses both the SPOF and the "do I trust this gateway's bytes" problem, and ArDrive is advertised as integrating it ([ar.io ArDrive case study](https://ar.io/case-studies/ardrive/)).

**Arweave base layer.** Public miner-concentration data is thin. A 2025 peer-reviewed paper claims the largest mining entity holds <1% of the network vs. Bitcoin's ~18% top pool, and ~25k nodes ([Wiley Engineering Reports, 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/eng2.70259)) — single-source, methodology not independently checked; flag as unverified. Protocol mechanics (full-weave packing incentives, dynamic block sizing) are designed to force replication spread. The doctrine that "you can always fetch chunks from miners" is true but has **no consumer tooling** — no ArDrive user will ever reassemble chunks by hand, so gateway-layer blocking is, for real users, effective censorship.

**Failure-mode matrix (what dies with what):**

| Dependency dies | ACCESS | DATA | WRITES |
|---|---|---|---|
| app.ardrive.io hosting | Degraded (use permaweb app copy / CLI) | Safe | Degraded |
| Turbo (bundler + payments) | Unaffected | Safe if settled; at-risk in unsettled window | Default path dead; credits lost; fallback = direct AR upload or other bundlers |
| arweave.net gateway | Major degradation until users/apps switch gateways (Wayfinder mitigates) | Safe | Default forwarding path dead |
| GraphQL indexes (arweave.net + Goldsky) | ArFS becomes *invisible* (can't list drives) even though bytes are on-chain | Safe | Unaffected |
| ArNS/AO registry malfunction | Named links break; txid links fine | Safe | Name management dead |
| PDS Inc. / ar.io team (the company) | All of the above at once, minus community gateways | Safe (this is the whole pitch) | Dead until community stands up bundlers/payment alternatives |
| Arweave network itself | Everything | Gone | Gone |

The honest headline: **DATA is genuinely decentralized; ACCESS is semi-centralized with a credible decentralization program; WRITES and PAYMENTS are a company product.**

## Strengths

- **Radical honesty about permanence and its limits.** The ToS states in plain (capitalized) language that nothing can be deleted, and the company's own COO published exactly how takedowns work, including the fact that blocked data remains fetchable. Compared to typical web3 hand-waving ("unstoppable!") this is best-in-class expectations-setting — legally self-protective *and* genuinely informative.
- **Moderation designed into the architecture rather than bolted on.** Per-gateway blocklists with an admin API, a `source` field anticipating shared feeds, jurisdiction-specific policy as an explicit operator right, and a phishing-detection sidecar — this is a coherent doctrine (data-layer neutrality + access-layer pluralism) that most decentralized-storage products lack entirely.
- **A real CSAM policy with NCMEC reporting.** Unusual for a web3 storage product to name the mechanism at all.
- **Everything that matters is open source and self-hostable** (AGPL bundler, gateway node, Wayfinder router), and the app itself has permaweb-deployed copies. The exit ramps from the company actually exist, which cannot be said of most "decentralized" products.
- **Wayfinder is a serious answer to the gateway SPOF** — client-side routing across community gateways plus hash verification of responses, shipping as SDK + extension + router.

## Weaknesses / user pain

- **Collateral over-blocking is the documented real-world failure, and it hits innocent users.** Shared-wallet DMCA blocklisting nuked bystanders' data availability; antivirus vendors flagging arweave.net broke NFT displays for whole platforms and some ISPs' customers. When it happens, the end user gets broken images with zero explanation.
- **No user-facing moderation transparency.** No public transparency report, no blocklist you can inspect as a user, no in-product "this content was blocked because X" UX found. The block simply looks like a missing file.
- **Turbo Credits are a custodial IOU dressed in crypto clothing**: non-refundable, expire after 3 years, ledger held by the company, share-only transferability. Total company-failure exposure for prepaid users, in a product whose whole pitch is outliving companies.
- **"Fetch from miners" is doctrine, not product.** There is no end-user tool to retrieve gateway-blocked content, so gateway blocking is de facto removal for ~everyone — the censorship-resistance claim rests on tooling that doesn't exist at the consumer level.
- **Permanence + reactive-only moderation is a footgun for the *uploader* too**: an accidental public upload of sensitive personal data is unremovable forever; the best the company offers is index delisting on their own infrastructure. Nothing in the product flow (beyond ToS text) makes this viscerally clear before the click. (App-level "hide" exists but is a cosmetic per-drive metadata flag, not removal.)
- **Traffic-share opacity.** 582 listed gateways is a fine headline; the product's defaults still funnel through arweave.net and company GraphQL, and AR.IO publishes no traffic distribution, so the practical decentralization of ACCESS is unquantifiable from outside.

## Implications for the EFS file browser

1. **Moderation lives at the access/index layer; keep the data layer neutral.** ArDrive's architecture validates EFS's existing ruling (no scheme allow/denylists on immutable contracts — ADR-0056): every workable takedown mechanism they have is at gateways/indexes/clients. EFS "Files" needs its own client-layer equivalent from day one: a hide/block list applied at render+listing time, published as an inspectable, forkable artifact — with better transparency than ArDrive's silent missing-file failure mode (show "blocked by <list> for <reason>, other clients may differ").
2. **A US-shipped client will be legally compelled the way ArDrive is.** Whoever hosts the EFS client and its default index needs a DMCA agent, an abuse contact, and a delisting procedure that operates on content *identifiers* while chain data persists. Write this policy before the first takedown letter, and copy ArDrive's ToS honesty about what deletion cannot do.
3. **ar:// mirrors inherit gateway-blocklist semantics.** An EFS file mirrored to ar:// can be reachable through one AR.IO gateway and blocked on another, or the whole default gateway can get vendor-flagged (Norton/Malwarebytes precedent). The SDK's fetch/resolution layer should treat gateway failure/blocking as a routine per-mirror condition — multi-gateway failover (Wayfinder-style, ideally with hash verification, which EFS gets naturally from content hashing) rather than a hardcoded gateway. This strengthens the "ar:// is a complement" story: EFS multi-mirror resolution is precisely the consumer tooling for blocked-content fallback that Arweave's ecosystem lacks.
4. **Avoid the shared-wallet collateral trap.** ArDrive's worst documented incident came from many users writing through one wallet, making one abuser's blocklisting everyone's outage. EFS's per-user attester model (wallet or app contract as attester, no shared relayer) is exactly the right shape — treat this as a hard requirement, since any future gasless/relayer convenience that funnels users through one on-chain identity recreates the trap.
5. **Make permanence a consent moment, not a ToS paragraph.** For any EFS write path that is practically unremovable (chain-anchored metadata, permanent mirrors), the Files app should surface irreversibility in the upload flow itself — ArDrive shows that all-caps legal text upstream of a drag-and-drop UI does not prevent the "I need this gone" support case.
6. **Publish an honest failure-mode map.** ArDrive's credibility comes from admitting DATA ≠ ACCESS ≠ WRITES. EFS should classify every dependency of the Files app (RPC, index, mirror carriers, resolver, any subsidized write path) into lose-ACCESS / lose-DATA / lose-WRITES and design prepaid/subsidized mechanisms (cf. Turbo Credits' custodial expiry) so user funds never die with a company.
