# Keep the parent out of the name

Prior systems and research clearly rule out pure path identity: a design where a topic *is* its path, such as `cars/tesla`, and the parent is permanently part of that id. Every strand of evidence counts against it. Strangers rarely agree even on one word. Professionals agree less when they must match a whole subdivided heading. About **30% of concepts in MeSH**, the US National Library of Medicine's subject vocabulary, have more than one parent. Systems that put the parent in the name could only reorganize by rewriting every record, or by getting a de facto authority to declare a flag day. Usenet, Obsidian and Logseq all show this, and Logseq changed course. What the evidence does favour is the shape used by mature systems such as MeSH, SKOS, Wikidata, AO3 and Hydrus. Identity is flat and stable. Placement, synonyms and merges are separate, revisable statements, ideally applied at read time. The hybrid is a tag that is a path of words, also indexed under each word. It is not a new idea: it closely resembles PRECIS and chain indexing, the British library schemes that filed one full subject string under every term in it. The evidence supports its useful part, which is pooling by word plus context that disambiguates. It also shows that the library world later moved away from stored subject strings. So the defensible design is a hybrid in which the **word or concept is the identity** and the path is only the tagger's attributed context. A path should not be a second permanent id that roll-up depends on. That conclusion is my inference from the evidence; no source tested it. The strongest case against flat words is that someone has to decide on the relations: every flat system studied needed an arbiter, and AO3 spends about 4.9 million volunteer decisions a year. The second strongest is that bare words are ambiguous.

## What "hierarchy in identity" means, and why the question matters

Some terms first. A **Schelling point** (or focal point) is the option that people who cannot talk to each other pick because it seems obvious. **Roll-up** means that a search for a parent also returns items tagged with its children. A search for `cars` that returns items tagged `cars/tesla` is an example. A **folksonomy** is a vocabulary built from users' free tags rather than by a curator. **Pre-coordination** means combining concepts into one stored string at tagging time (`cars/tesla`, or the library heading "France—History—1789–1799"). **Post-coordination** means storing separate terms and combining them at search time (`cars` AND `tesla`). **Polyhierarchy** means a concept can have several parents. **Inter-indexer consistency** measures how often two trained cataloguers assign the same terms to the same item. Most studies use Hooper's measure: shared terms divided by all terms used by either person.

Three designs are in play. In (I), the path is the identity. In (II), words are flat ids and "parent", "synonym" and "moved to" are statements anyone can make. In (III), a tag is a path of words, indexed under every prefix and under every word. The requirements narrow the field: no authority can rename anything, nobody's statement is ever rewritten, and curation exists only as overlays each reader chooses (EFS calls these lenses). Any system that repaired its mistakes by rewriting records has no lesson to offer except a cautionary one.

## Strangers rarely agree on one word, and agree less on whole strings

The baseline is bleak. In Furnas, Landauer, Gomez and Dumais's 1987 "vocabulary problem" study, two people chose the same term for the same thing with **probability below 0.20 in all five domains** tested. If access depended on a designer's single favourite word, it failed **80–90%** of the time. The authors' remedy was "unlimited aliasing", meaning many names for one target, not one enforced name ([abstract via Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/00f34424be4f1ca2183d8690680d8d28); [DBLP](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/cacm/FurnasLGD87.html)). A 1996 review by Brennan and Clark gives the range as **.07 to .18**, and adds that even 20 aliases for one command covered two people's choices only about 80% of the time. That figure is secondary; the review itself was not opened ([Brennan 1996](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/brennan96.pdf)). In an unverified CiteULike study, only **946 of 4,638 tags** were used by two or more people ([search summary](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/26459/bitstreams/90319/data.pdf)).

Convergence happens anyway, and it comes from *seeing* other people's choices, not from the form of the name. Golder and Huberman, who read the full text, found that on del.icio.us, after "the first 100 or so bookmarks", each tag's share of a URL's tags stays nearly fixed ([Golder & Huberman 2006](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/GolderAndHuberman-usage%20patterns%20collab%20tagging.pdf)). Every generative model of this effect relies on imitation ([Cattuto et al., PNAS 2007](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0605015)). The cleanest experiment is Centola and Baronchelli's 2015 naming game with real people. In "well-mixed" groups, where everyone can meet everyone, success reached **100%** and the dominant name was used by about **96%** of players. In spatial networks, where each player saw only neighbours, success never rose above 75% and the leading name held about **30%** ([PNAS 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4343158)). A 2018 follow-up found that a committed minority of about **25%** could overturn an established convention, while 21% could not ([Science SM](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Centola-et-al.-2018-Science.-Tipping-Point-Supplementary-Materials.pdf)). One inference follows for EFS. Whether strangers converge depends more on whether the client shows everyone the same tags already in use than on how tag ids are built.

Does agreement fall as a tag gets more levels? No study tests this with lay taggers. The pre/post-coordination researcher concluded the evidence "likely does not exist". The nearest evidence comes from professional cataloguers, and it all points one way. Tonta (1991) compared Library of Congress and British Library cataloguers assigning LCSH headings to 82 books. They matched the **full subdivided string 16%** of the time, and matched **36%** when a missing subdivision was forgiven, as long as the main heading matched. The 36% figure also forgave some synonyms ([Tonta, LRTS 1991](http://eprints.rclis.org/9464/1/indxcons.pdf)). In a MEDLINE study of 760 articles each indexed twice, Funk and Reid (1983) found that consistency was **lowered when subheadings were attached** to a main heading. Free-floating subheadings were more consistent than attached ones ([PMC227138](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC227138)). In LILACS, agreement on qualifiers was only **11%**, against 36.6% on descriptors ([Information Research 2013](https://informationr.net/ir/18-4/paper601.html)). Agreement is highest for the smallest, coarsest vocabularies: MEDLINE "checktags" reached **74.7%** ([Mork et al. 2017](https://jbiomedsem.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13326-017-0113-5)). The rule that per-level agreement multiplies across levels (roughly p^k for a k-level path) is simple probability that assumes the levels are independent. It is not a measured result.

Retrieval tests agree. In Cranfield II (1963–66), 33 index languages were compared on aeronautics papers. **Single words ranked first** (normalised recall 65.82) and pre-coordinated "simple concepts" ranked last (44.64). The authors judged concepts over-specific and single terms "about right". Pre-coordinated concepts did buy precision: **50.9% precision at 14.1% recall**, against **14.8% precision at 66.7% recall** for single terms ([Spärck Jones pp. 277–278](https://www-nlpir.nist.gov/projects/irlib/pubs/ire/ire_text/pg_278.txt)). That trade-off is the core of this decision. Paths make a tag specific, and words make it findable.

## Every mature system keeps a stable id apart from a movable position

The clearest precedent is MeSH. NLM says its tree numbers, the codes that place a descriptor in the hierarchy, "have no intrinsic significance" and are "subject to change". A descriptor "may appear in as many additional places as may be appropriate" ([NLM](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/intro_trees.html)). The research team parsed the 2026 descriptor file directly and found **31,110 descriptors, a mean of 2.10 tree numbers each, and 30.1% with two or more distinct parents**. The often-quoted 53% overstates this because tree numbers are inherited from parents that appear in several places. Even so, 8.5% of descriptors span more than one top-level category ([NLM MeSH files](https://nlmpubs.nlm.nih.gov/projects/mesh/MESH_FILES/xmlmesh/); team's own computation, with no NLM figure to cross-check). PubMed's "explode" feature still gives roll-up: a search on a descriptor automatically includes the narrower descriptors under it, computed from the tree at query time ([NLM retrieval](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/intro_retrieval.html)). This is a working example of roll-up over a hierarchy that is not part of identity.

SKOS, the W3C standard for publishing thesauri, works the same way. Concepts are URIs. `broader` is an asserted, revisable link. More than one path is explicitly allowed, and cycles are consistent with the data model ([W3C SKOS](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/)). Wikidata keeps an old Q-id working after a merge by turning it into a redirect, because third parties depend on stable ids ([Phabricator T40664](https://phabricator.wikimedia.org/T40664)). Tim Berners-Lee's "Cool URIs don't change" gives the rationale: "when you use a topic name in a URI you are binding yourself to some classification" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/Provider/Style/URI); paraphrased by the fetch tool). The DOI Handbook calls DOI names "dumb numbers" from which nothing may be inferred ([DOI Handbook](https://albany.doi.org/doi_handbook/2_Numbering.html)). There is one dissent: a Crossref forum post argues that opacity harms linking ([Crossref community](https://community.crossref.org/t/opaque-dois-considered-harmful/13788)).

Hierarchy does appear inside identifiers, but mainly to **delegate ownership**. MIME's vendor and personal trees hand a sub-space to a registrant ([RFC 6838](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6838)). Java package names borrow the domain-name registry ([Oracle](https://docs.oracle.com/javase/tutorial/java/package/namingpkgs.html)). In ENS, the parent's hash is an input to the child's hash because the parent controls its subnames ([ENS docs](https://docs.ens.domains/resolution/names)). The one MIME tree with no authority, `x.`, cannot be registered and is discouraged. Older library classifications are the exception to the pattern. In Dewey and Library of Congress Classification, the notation *is* the position. One library's reclassification of **69,879 volumes cost about $585,100**, roughly $8 a volume, a cost created by physical shelves and labels ([search summary](https://paw.princeton.edu/article/firestone-phasing-out-uniquely-princeton-way-classify-books); not fetched). In 2005 Shirky argued that single placement is a legacy of the one-shelf rule and that hierarchy suits "small, stable, formal domains with expert catalogers" ([Shirky](https://www.shirky.com/writings/ontology_overrated.html)).

## Tag products show that a path in the name forces rewrites

The product evidence converges on the same point. Obsidian and Bear store `#a/b` as literal text, so roll-up by prefix is free. A rename, though, means editing every note. Obsidian's Tag Wrangler plugin (1.1M downloads) warns that the rename is "potentially an irreversible operation" and that sync can leave it half-done ([plugin page](https://community.obsidian.md/plugins/tag-wrangler)). **Logseq changed course.** In its database version, namespaced pages "no longer have their namespace embedded in their page name". Tags can have several parents through `Extends`. Previously a namespace change "could only be done by renaming" ([Logseq docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/logseq/docs/master/db-version-changes.md)). Evernote gives a tag an opaque `guid` and an optional `parentGuid` ([Evernote](https://dev.evernote.com/doc/reference/Types.html)). Gmail gives a label an immutable id and a display name that contains "/" ([Gmail API](https://developers.google.com/workspace/gmail/api/reference/rest/v1/users.labels)). Gmail has no roll-up, though: applying `Foo/Bar` does not make a message match `Foo` ([gmailctl #324](https://github.com/mbrt/gmailctl/issues/324)).

Stack Exchange declined hierarchy outright. Jeff Atwood said in 2010: "I don't think adding this kind of complexity would make the tag system better" ([MSE 58073](https://meta.stackexchange.com/questions/58073)). Users raised the main objection themselves: most tags have more than one plausible parent, such as C# under .NET or Mono ([MSE 45438](https://meta.stackexchange.com/questions/45438)). Today Stack Overflow has **65,783 tags and 5,332 synonyms** ([SE API](https://api.stackexchange.com/2.3/tags/synonyms?site=stackoverflow&filter=total)). Its lesson for a permanent ledger is direct. A synonym redirect can be undone. A merge, which retags every question, is "not (easily) reversible" ([MSE 70710](https://meta.stackexchange.com/questions/70710)).

The image boards and AO3 split on whether the record is preserved. **Danbooru and e621 rewrite posts.** Danbooru's alias code "retags the posts themselves" ([tag_mover.rb](https://github.com/danbooru/danbooru/blob/master/app/logical/tag_mover.rb)). e621 keeps undo snapshots because the rewrite is destructive ([e621ng](https://github.com/e621ng/e621ng/blob/master/app/models/tag_alias.rb)). **Hydrus does not rewrite.** Siblings and parents are applied at display time; the client "secretly remember[s]" the original tag, and "P is not actually added to every file". Each user ranks which services' relations apply ([Hydrus siblings](https://hydrusnetwork.github.io/hydrus/advanced_siblings.html); [parents](https://hydrusnetwork.github.io/hydrus/advanced_parents.html)). Of all the systems, Hydrus is the closest match to per-reader lenses. Its documentation also warns against "perfect" hierarchies, because reasonable people disagree about parents. **AO3** promises "we will never change the tags on a work or bookmark", with narrow exceptions ([AO3 Tags FAQ](https://archiveofourown.org/faq/tags)). Behind the scenes, though, it materializes roll-up at write time: a background job rebuilds a `FilterTagging` table for every affected work, then reindexes search ([filter_updater.rb](https://github.com/otwcode/otwarchive/blob/master/app/models/filter_updater.rb)).

## Usenet is the only large-scale test of ownerless path names

Usenet is the closest real precedent for design (I) at scale, and it cuts both ways. Hierarchical group names worked as shared addresses for decades. The ISC `active` file still lists **45,002 groups**, of which **20,466 are alt.\*** (team's count, 2026-11-25; [ISC active](https://ftp.isc.org/pub/usenet/CONFIG/active)). Reorganizing them was another matter. In 1985 Mark Horton said that renaming net.columbia would be "politically... very hard" ([Toronto archive](https://ftp.cs.toronto.edu/doc/usenet.hist/rename)). The Great Renaming of 1986–87 happened only because a "Backbone Cabal" of high-connectivity sites, which "had no power de jure", could starve a group of distribution ([Bellovin 2019](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~smb/blog/2019-12/2019-12-26.html)). Horton's final scheme mapped **242 old names onto 222 new ones**. Continuity came only from a local alias file that each site installed: seismo's had **271 mappings** by May 1987, and it already corrected the "final" plan ([Great Renaming lists](http://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://www.panix.com/~josephb/newsgroups/history/rename-rmgroup/Great-Renaming-lists.txt)). The position in the tree carried consequences: "moving something to the talk hierarchy would sharply curtail its distribution" (Bellovin). Decades later, soc.culture.jewish's FAQ still has to tell readers to stop arguing from the group's name ([SCJ FAQ](https://ftp.lip6.fr/pub/doc/faqs/judaism/FAQ/01-FAQ-intro)).

The alt.\* hierarchy is the authority-free version. Its FAQ says alt groups "can't be removed or even re-named. Alt groups never die, they just fade away" ([alt creation guide](https://grahammitchell.com/usenet/newalt.html)). ISC's shared policy file accepts every alt newgroup and ignores every alt rmgroup, noting that "tons of alt.\* newgroups are sent out regularly with the intent more to create nonsense entries" ([control.ctl](https://ftp.isc.org/pub/usenet/CONFIG/control.ctl)). The tree also became a vector for abuse: random-string names up to **21 components deep**, and a 9-component harassment chain under alt.config. Shared names still formed, but through social filtering such as alt.config review and admin choice, not through the namespace. A 2000–01 Microsoft Research study found **2,381 active groups with "comp" in their name, but only 1,171 in comp.\***. It concluded that the name hierarchy "is sometimes uninformative, inconsistent, or even deliberately misleading" ([Fiore & Smith](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2001-94.doc)). That pair of figures is the strongest single argument in the notes for indexing under each word. Half the computing groups sat outside the computing branch, and only the shared word "comp" connects them.

## The hybrid has a real ancestor, and that ancestor was retired

Design (III) has a direct historical parallel. Ranganathan's chain indexing, and then Derek Austin's PRECIS, filed a subject string so that **every access term leads to an entry with the full subject in context**. PRECIS was trialled on about 95,000 British National Bibliography records in 1971–73 and was replaced by COMPASS in 1996, and later by LCSH ([ISKO: PRECIS](https://isko.org/cyclo/precis)). The broader trend has been to break strings apart. OCLC's FAST keeps LCSH's words but drops its string syntax, so non-specialists can apply it: Cornell trained cataloguers in about **10 minutes** ([Hanging Together](https://hangingtogether.org/faceted-vocabularies/)). In December 2015 NLM stopped distributing pre-built MeSH strings because "long subject strings do not work well" for linked data ([NLM 2015](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/so15/so15_cataloging_unstringing_subject_heading.html)). NLM kept topical subheadings attached to their main headings and split out only place and form. Pre-coordination still has serious defenders. Thomas Mann of the Library of Congress argues that word order carries meaning Boolean AND cannot capture, and that browse displays let researchers "recognize" options they could not have specified ([Mann 2000](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/bibcontrol/mann_paper.pdf)). LC kept pre-coordination in 2007 on those grounds, but cited no measured data ([LC](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/cpso/pre_vs_post.html)).

Taken together, the evidence suggests (my inference) that a hybrid works when the stored string is context and the access points are the words, and fails when the string is the unit everyone must agree on. That favours a particular version of (III). The concept word is the identity. Each tag statement may carry an ordered path as the tagger's own attributed context, filed under each word. Parent, synonym and "moved to" are separate lens statements, as in (II). Prefix roll-up along paths can be kept as a convenience, but should not be the main way a parent finds its children, because Tonta's 16% and the Netscan figures show full paths are rarely shared. Two failure modes remain. If the path itself becomes a linkable permanent id, (III) inherits the failure modes of (I). If roll-up depends on the path, `cars/tesla` and `companies/tesla` produce two incompatible trees.

## The strongest arguments against the favoured design

The first objection is that relations need an arbiter, and a ledger has none. In every flat system studied, synonyms and parents were settled by someone: AO3's roughly 400 wranglers, Stack Exchange's reputation votes (suggestions need 2,500 rep, approval at +4), Danbooru approvers, Hydrus janitors on the shared repository. AO3 wrangled **about 4,944,000 tags in 2025**, roughly 1,300 per volunteer, and more than 32 million since 2009 (snippets; [Jan 2026 newsletter](https://archiveofourown.org/admin_posts/34226); [Oct 2026 post](https://archiveofourown.org/admin_posts/36276)). New tags sometimes took "up to a month" to wrangle ([admin post 12131](https://archiveofourown.org/admin_posts/12131)). With lenses and no shared curator, the default reader sees no merges at all unless a trusted lens supplies them. Design (I) needs no curation for roll-up, which Obsidian demonstrates. Mitigations: AO3 reports that unwrangled tags still work in search and filters (an unverified summary), and Centola's results suggest a visible shared default could carry most of the load.

The second objection is ambiguity. A bare word pools unrelated meanings: "tesla" the car company, the unit and the inventor. Uniterm, the first post-coordinate system (1951), was criticized from the start for "false drops", where terms combined at search time described unrelated concepts ([Wikipedia: Uniterm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniterm)). AO3 and Danbooru, both flat, still had to write disambiguation into names, as in "Bianca (Fandom)" and `fate_(series)`, because a flat global key cannot otherwise tell the meanings apart ([AO3 FAQ](https://archiveofourown.org/faq/tags); [Danbooru](https://danbooru.donmai.us/wiki_pages/help:tag_implications)). This is the best evidence for keeping *some* context next to the word. It is not evidence for putting the parent in the identity. Qualifiers in these systems mark *which concept* is meant, not *where it sits* in a tree. Cranfield's single terms still scored best overall despite poor precision, so the notes do not show word pooling to be unusable.

The third objection is that open parent statements decay. English Wikipedia's categories contain about **3,000 to 4,100 cycles**; the two counts were not reconciled ([Wishlist W281](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Community_Wishlist/W281/en); [T263096](https://phabricator.wikimedia.org/T263096)). Wikidata's subclass hierarchy has documented loops and redundant links ([arXiv 2411.15550](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15550v1)). In a lens model, each reader's roll-up must tolerate cycles and conflicting parents, and a crowd-sourced default lens would inherit Wikipedia's problems. The fourth objection is that flat words lose browsable places and word order. Mann's recognition argument and the 50.9% precision of pre-coordinated concepts are real. FAST adopters reported "more irrelevant results" and weak browse displays. The fifth objection is about normalization: whatever text is hashed into an id is frozen forever. ENSIP-15 had to keep a legacy quirk, ignoring emoji presentation characters, because "registration hashes are immutable" ([ENSIP-15](https://docs.ens.domains/ensip/15)). This applies to words and paths alike, but a path freezes more choices.

## Gaps that limit confidence

Several holes matter for this decision. No study measures how often lay strangers agree on multi-level path tags against single words. The penalty for depth is extrapolated from professional cataloguers and from probability. No source describes a deployed, ownerless, path-hashed topic scheme, or any append-only roll-up index. The cost and abuse profile of filing every tag under every word and prefix is untested; the sealed file itself names this as the contracts dev's question. Several AO3 figures and the Stack Exchange synonym statistics come from search snippets or mirrors. Bowker and Star's *Sorting Things Out*, Weinberger's book in detail, and faceted-search usability studies (Hearst) were not reached. So the claims about faceted navigation rest on definitions, not measurements.

## Conclusion

The question shifts once the evidence is in. It stops being "path or flat?" and becomes "what is the unit strangers must agree on, and who resolves disagreement?" On the first, the research is consistent: the smaller and coarser the unit, the more agreement there is. On the second, a ledger has no answer except visibility and lenses, and Centola's experiments suggest visibility matters more than id design. That turns a protocol choice partly into a client choice. Show everyone the same existing tags by default, or convergence will look like the spatial-network groups, where the leading name held 30%, not the well-mixed ones at 96%.

The design that fits the evidence keeps concepts (words, with disambiguating qualifiers where needed) as permanent identity. It keeps a path as attributed context indexed under each word, and keeps every parent, synonym and move as a revisable, per-reader statement. The precedents for each piece are named above: MeSH's stable id and movable tree number, PRECIS's filing under every term, Hydrus's non-destructive display-time relations, and Usenet's lesson that anything placed in a name needs a flag day to change. The open risk is not structural; it is social. Every flat system that worked had people deciding synonyms and parents. EFS has to show that lenses can supply that work without anyone holding authority over it.
